Research

Probabilistic and analytic number theory, log-correlated fields, and the estimation problems that come with them.

Current Direction

My current research involves log-correlated fields: Gaussian fields whose covariance diverges logarithmically on the diagonal. They appear across 2D quantum gravity, hydrodynamic turbulence and energy dissipation, multifractal volatility modeling, and random matrix theory for signal processing.

Exponentiating a log-correlated Gaussian field gives Gaussian multiplicative chaos, a random measure with no pointwise definition, built as a limit of regularizations[3]. It is the standard model of multifractal scaling: the exponent of the q-th moment of the mass of a small ball is nonlinear in q.

Integers are deterministic but behave pseudo-randomly. The logarithm of a zeta function near the critical line is log-correlated, and Bailey and Keating's survey[4] treats both sides at once: the probabilistic theory of these fields, and their arithmetic realizations. Both halves of my current work come from there.

The arithmetic half: covariance of Dedekind zeta functions

By Artin factorization, the Dedekind zeta function of a number field splits into L-functions attached to the irreducible representations of its Galois closure, each raised to a multiplicity, so two fields in the same tower share constituents. I am computing the covariance of the logarithms of two such Dedekind zeta functions on the critical line.

The leading term is the inner product of the two multiplicity vectors, which counts the shared constituents. For the S₃ tower generated by a non-Galois cubic field, the four covariances assemble into the Gram matrix of those vectors. At that order the fluctuation law is universal and the count is the only arithmetic input, so my question is what survives at the next order, and whether the lower-order terms retain field-specific data such as conductors and ramification.

The estimation half: where measured exponents stop being trustworthy

Deriving a scaling law is not the same as measuring one. Where the moments converge, the multifractal exponents are known in closed form. Estimating them from a finite record is a separate problem, and it belongs to the signal processing literature, where multifractal analysis developed.

Two obstructions are documented there[5]. Past a critical order the measured exponent curve straightens into a line; that order is a property of the process and does not shrink as the record grows. A second critical moment depends on sample size and can be estimated from a finite record. I have found neither worked out for Gaussian multiplicative chaos as the kernel varies, and I expect the intrinsic order to be fixed by the intermittency parameter alone while the sample-size order drifts with record length, so that the two breaks separate under simulation.

Testing this means modeling and simulation at known parameters across kernels, scale ranges and record lengths. The exponents come from regression of log structure functions on log scale, one slope per moment order q, and locating the critical orders — where the resulting curve in q leaves its predicted shape — can be done by computational data analysis.

Record length is the binding constraint. The scaling has to be visible across several decades of scale before the exponent curve separates from its own finite-size bias, and the high-q end needs enough record to contain events that are rare by construction. That threshold is what tail estimation needs: a return period is a claim about a distribution tail, and the order at which the estimate fails bounds how far the claim can be pushed.

First Graduate Project

What machine learning can recover from congruence data alone

My first graduate research project asked how much of an integer's multiplicative structure machine learning models can recover from congruence data alone, without factorization. With Yangbo Ye I trained random forest and logistic regression classifiers to pick out r-almost primes — integers with exactly r prime factors counted with multiplicity — for r = 2 through 8. We trained near 244 using integer-vector encodings, then tested without retraining near 2176, running the pipeline in PySpark on the university's Argon advanced computing cluster.

Random forests beat the baseline at the training scale for every r, but only r = 2 and r = 3 survived the jump in scale. Removing small prime factors from the training set and repeating the experiment gave null results, which implies the classifiers were finding divisibility by small primes rather than multiplicative structure.

Two independent papers point the same way. Lowry-Duda[1] found that transformers predict the squarefree indicator but not the Möbius sign, and traced the gap to divisibility by small squares. Pozdnyakov[2] proved that, under certain conditions, broad classes of learning algorithms cannot weak-learn Möbius or Liouville from residue data.

Undergraduate

  • Failed Zero Forcing Numbers of Trees and Circulant Graphs
    With K. Rubi, J. Terrazas, R. Flórez, D. A. Narayan. Theory and Applications of Graphs, Vol. 11, Iss. 1, Art. 5, 2025
    We investigate the failed zero forcing number F(G) — the largest size of a vertex set that does not force the entire graph under the zero forcing rule. For trees, we prove the lower bound F(T) ≥ α(T) − 1 relating F to the independence number, and we determine F(G) exactly for several families of 4- and 6-regular circulant graphs C_n{1,k}, C_n{1,2}, C_n{1,3}, and C_n{1,2,3} using extremal constructions and pigeonhole arguments.

  • All Graphs with a Failed Zero Forcing Number of Two
    With K. Rubi, J. Terrazas, D. A. Narayan. Symmetry, 13, 2221, 2021
    We completely characterize the graphs G with failed zero forcing number F(G) = 2, resolving a problem posed by Fetcie, Jacob, and Saavedra in 2015. The characterization identifies exactly 15 such graphs, and we prove that every graph on at least 7 vertices satisfies F(G) ≥ 3 via a reduction argument that rules out pendant-V and pendant-K₃ obstructions.
